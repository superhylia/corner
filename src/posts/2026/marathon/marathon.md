I’m not much of a fan of shooters. At least, I thought so. Growing up, shooters were “The Game.” After *Halo: Combat Evolved* (2001) shaped expectations for the genre on consoles, the massive critical and financial successes of both *Halo 3* (2007) and *Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare* (2007) set the first-person shooter’s dominance in motion. Just as the platformer had before it, the “machine” kept turning, slowly homogenizing a genre into one style, one audience, and one goal: money. Admittedly, it’s a harsh outlook to have, but fatigue with the genre set in very quickly, even at a young age, as series like *CoD*, its competitors and copycats alike, released every year without fail. I let the genre pass me by as a blur of car hums, grey skies, and brownish trees. The genre just wasn’t for me, and I didn’t think it ever would be.

Fast forward a few years and my stance started to change. *Splatoon* (2015) came out and ran counter to every expectation I had for the genre: it was Nintendo, it was weird, and for god’s sake, it was colorful. I don’t play as much as I used to, but *Splatoon 2* (2017) got quite a few hours out of me, with *Octo Expansion* (2018) piquing my interest when I get the time. *Metroid Prime* (2002), while not having a strong combat focus, blew the doors off what a shooter *could* be and remains one of the finest-designed games ever put on disc. Lost in the blur of ignoring the genre were great campaigns I nervously dipped my toe into. My first experiences with the *Halo* trilogy, *Vanquish* (2010), and especially *Titanfall 2* (2016) made them some of my favorite games of all time, with packed pacing, movement mechanics, and great gunplay. There were shooters I liked now!

When *Marathon* (2026) was first revealed, I knew very little about the series' lineage beyond its status as an [Apple Pippin oddity](https://breezewiki.com/pippin/wiki/Super_Marathon) and Mac classic. Given Sony’s track record with multiplayer shooters, I, like many others, did not have high hopes for the game. Expectations certainly weren’t helped by Bungie’s continued mismanagement, tarnished reputation, and now even plagiarism, marring the game's nascent reception. It would probably pass me by again as yet another multiplayer title for someone primarily into crafted solo experiences. But something about it stuck out to me…

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXhQbF7TlbA

It had art direction. A strange, strange world of danger, organically robotic. The colors were so stark, almost plastic in their sheen. It was striking. In a AAA games sphere that consistently prioritizes graphical fidelity above all else, *Marathon* had real character to its look. A friend of mine, deeply in love with Bungie’s past works, encouraged our group to try the game out during the Server Slam. Outside of fighting games, *Among Us* (2018), and the occasional *Monster Hunter* *Rise* (2021) session, I rarely ever played games online with others, competitively or cooperatively. Fears of my inability to play well or contribute to my team kept me at a distance from the genre, so I only tackled it alone. My friends pushed me, though, and having nothing to lose, I gave it an honest shot.

## Gameplay: Death is the First Step.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/leap.jpg", "A Vandal Shell leaps from a high point.", null, "[Bungie](https://press.bungie.com/Marathon#?tab=screenshots-1&scrollto=)." %}

As one of eight unique Shell characters in *Marathon*, the player explores the item-laden, hazardous maps of extrasolar colony Tau Ceti IV against other players and UESC enemies (PvPvE) in 25-minute runs. Runners risk time and inventory to uncover gear for empowered combat, complete narrative Contract missions for capability-enhancing factions, and reach an ‘exfil’ extraction point to keep found spoils. As someone inexperienced with online shooters, the extraction shooter genre, and competitive multiplayer games as a whole, I found *Marathon* entrancing in the most alien ways. *Marathon* is oppressive, flowing, and tactical. Bungie’s gunplay continues to be the best in the industry: every weapon and throwable has its own kinesthetic identity that goes beyond abilities and range. Machine guns feel heavy, shotguns feel nimble, and the Hardline PR feels like a dogshit weapon to use without upgrades, every bullet choking out of its barrel. Tau Ceti IV is ruthless, bordering on survival horror in solo play. Every shot ringing out behind you could be a bot, a turret, or a player, and with time-to-kill so quick in this game, one wrong move can truly be the end of a run. Within that ruthlessness comes *Marathon*’s greatest strength: its emergent gameplay. Emergent gameplay has quite a [few](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_gameplay) [different](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/examining-emergent-gameplay) [definitions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jWEuSV_nbfA), but I feel a remarkable moment from one of my favorite games ever made shows it to you.

https://youtu.be/9SNrFaXCm1E

In *The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom* (2023), Link has access to 5 Zonai abilities that allow him to terraform and interact with the environment around him. Typically, when fighting the recurring Flux Guardians, I use the same surefire strategy of bomb arrows to sink it closer to the ground and then Ascend through it to hit its core weak point-rinse and repeat. On this attempt, however, I accidentally used Rewind during the Guardian’s spread attack (you can see my confusion by attempting both Ascend and Rewind again) and realized: *I can ride the blocks up.* This moment exemplifies both how much I love the *Breath of the Wild* duology and what makes *Marathon* shine: the interaction of separate mechanics that, dev-intended or not, creates complex outcomes and creative strategies.

The nature of extraction shooters is a volatile dance between risk and reward. Fearing loss, I fell back on Sponsored Kits from the Armory. What seems like a low-risk, high-reward option becomes a crucial lesson in adaptability and comfort, a vestige of *Zelda*’s weapon durability that I defend, if not at least understand. Relying on a basic loadout of low-end weapons and unextractable consumables puts the player at a disadvantage compared to those who risk better loadouts. Bring little to nothing into a run? A few shots is all it’ll take to send you back to the lobby. Carry too much? Run out of inventory for even better scraps of material. Hoard your loot? Vault space can fill much faster than upgrades will expand it, forcing you to chance the stuff you’ve meticulously collected in the next run and maybe even leave some of it behind. Push on a trio or take on a Warden? The rich may only get richer with your signature on the invoice in blue blood. Come in loaded, stay longer, and go farther on the toughest maps for the best of the best pickups, but face one bad encounter and gamble everything you’ve found and everything you’ve started with. Dependency becomes futile.

The brilliance of *Marathon* is that all of this emergence, born from risk and reward, extends to every stage of the game for every skill level. As you get further into the match, your affordances grow: there are fewer players rotating the map, meaning previously untenable points of interest (POI) are now within your grasp. If you can stick the run out, coming in with better gear can heavily tip the scales in early engagement. Special keys unlock hidden areas or lockbox chests for your troubles. Coordination with your squad and maximizing each Shell’s abilities mix into a concoction of flanking and pressure that can pay out big. Decisions become playbooks, pressure becomes confidence, and the environment around you becomes a tool in your arsenal. UESC aren’t only foes, but friends if their attention is on another group: who's to say you can’t cause that? The player becomes familiar with the rotating weapons provided by the sponsored kits, how they’ll execute, and grows their strategy from there. Eventually, the shell takes its first flight away from the nest, going further and further into the wild with every take-off to forage and collect.

## Progression: Expect Death. Embrace Rebirth.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/titanfall.png", "A screenshot of *Titanfall* during it's second campaign level, The Colony. An NPC, Graves, appears on a comms line in the right corner: 'It's the voice of a dead man. Find out where it's coming from.'", "The Colony campaign level in *Titanfall*.", "[APE CHANNEL.](https://youtu.be/3QRONZAZ7ug?si=MeehWyMXsDVStA6Z)" %}

After delivering a once-in-a-generation title in *Call of Duty 4*: *Modern Warfare* (2007), Infinity Ward’s founders Jason West and the late Vince Zampella wanted more creative control and bonuses from their contracts. Activision shakily agreed on two terms: they would develop *Modern Warfare 2* (2009), and control would revert if the duo ever left, from what I’m sure Bobby Kotick called “unforeseen circumstances.” A few wrongful termination lawsuits, a 46-person exodus, and a deal with Electronic Arts later, the two formed Respawn Entertainment and produced *Titanfall* (2013). Highly anticipated, *Titanfall*, as Zampella put it, brought “scale, verticality, and story” to the genre with a unique proposition: what if the campaign *was* the multiplayer? Players would experience a nine-mission campaign across two team-based game types through interstitial dialogue, two factions, and specialized map variants featuring scripted setpieces like dragons and escape sequences.

The multiplayer campaign didn’t receive the greatest reception. Reviews found it shallow, dull, and poorly voiced, no more than background noise to what was otherwise a frenetic and quality shooter. Despite a big launch, players fell off the game, while [curious buyers were averse](https://www.polygon.com/2016/8/18/12533412/titanfall-2-single-player-demo-gamescom-2016/) to a full-price package without a complete solo mode to fall back on. This feedback eventually led to *Titanfall 2*’s celebrated campaign, marrying Titan setpieces with terrific traversal, phenomenal puzzle-solving, and [legendary level design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effect_and_Cause). A thousand miles away and a generation later, Bungie found themselves asking the same questions Respawn did. How do you weave a harrowing sci-fi scare of corporatized mercenary research and failed expeditions in a way that affects matches and players?

Contracts and the Codex were their answer, and, in my eyes, *Marathon*’s biggest accomplishment. Offered by one of six Factions, Contracts bestow loot, reputation for Faction upgrade trees, and one-time Priority Contracts that serve as narrative vignettes into Tau Ceti IV. Boosts to vitals, enhancements to abilities, and access to items are offered by the mercy of Monsanto-like mega corporations, death cultists, radical terrorists, and even the makers of the Shells themselves. From defeating a set of enemies with specific weapons to carrying and installing implants across the map, these objectives range in zone and condition. All of this then feeds into the Codex, which rewards you with free cosmetics, occasional loot, and lore entries to read and listen to that further develop the world. Everything serves to benefit, and for someone new to the genre, these became my foundation. Consistent, variable objectives that advance the narrative and progression on every run, whether successful or failed, gave me room to push further. Early exits stung, but never scarred. Exfils felt more rewarding than just “surviving” or even “winning,” but experiencing. It was progress.

Cast into an ever-changing shadow of a body destined for killing, there’s a strange illumination that still shines on the story that only a Shell could provide. Thinking back on one of my favorite Contracts, I realize that very little of each of *Marathon*’s zones is occupied by anything resembling a human: there are bots, there are plants, but no standing life to occupy it. The times something does stand, it's another player governed by the whims of whatever feeds their hunger best; evidently, I became very enticing to Shell and Scout alike. When I went to complete NuCaloric’s Paradigm Shift 1/5, I scanned samples in low tides across the east side of Perimeter as trigger-happy UESC nipped at my heels, eager to drown me. The music tensed as I snuck through the tunnels as other players stamped to gain riches above and behind me. Against the clock, having to cover so much distance, my Vandal rushed to the East Wall only to find something that resembled…me? It spoke.

“Endlessly mutating killer” is fitting for what I became. A movement junkie, my shell Vandal felt smooth but hard to parse in what her kit provided. I died a lot. Lost a lot of good stuff. A Hardline PR can send you back to homelessness very quickly. Eventually, though, Perimeter felt like my world to conquer. I learned that Disrupt Cannon can push enemies away from cover at the right angle. Footsteps became my metronome as the bushes of Dire Marsh became my home. I started playing solo, and the fear of letting my trio down no longer felt as malignant when I was playing more than them. Greed comes for everyone, and “just one more key” in Outpost cleared a lot of my inventory. Like an animal, I went back to hunt. To flank, to push, to hide, and to run: these are the lessons of the wilderness that became installed into me, a Shell of myself, one that itched and scratched for just one more run. As I closed the game for the night, I winced at my Steam hours, not used to seeing triple digits in only 2 months. The button still hovered over “PLAY”. Maybe I was a fan of shooters after all.

## Season 2 (So Far): Somewhere in the Heavens…

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/sentinel.jpg", "A render of the Sentinel Shell introduced in Season 2: Nightfall. Sentinel is holding a gun center-frame in front of a red sky and green aurora.", null, "[Bungie](https://press.bungie.com/Marathon#?tab=screenshots-1&scrollto=)." %}

This essay took me a long time to write, initially drafting my thoughts on the game during the pre-launch Server Slam playtest. 100+ hours later, this game has sucked me into its gravitational pull in a way very few games in recent memory have. As my friends gradually ramped on and off the game, I kept extracting almost every day. Just one more run before bed. One more run till the end of this podcast. One more run. The fear of futility to my team and for my improvement had finally washed away. While worries about the game's future have always been an unfortunate downer to this game's reputation, things looked up for *Marathon*. Season 2: Nightfall brought a brand-new map in Night Marsh, the Sentinel Shell, major improvements like the Cradle, Sponsored Survival for PVP-lite, and a free weekend to get billions on the game. A firesale of all of my loot in some of the most fun matches I’ve had with the game as the clock ticked closer to the reset. I couldn’t have been more excited for June 2nd.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/something_went_wrong.png", "A screenshot of *Marathon* with a monitor stating AN ERROR HAS OCCURED. SOMETHING WENT WRONG.", "Admittedly, the actual errors never looked this cool.", "My screenshot of *Marathon*." %}

The day came. [So did the jungle animals.](https://help.marathonthegame.com/hc/en-us/sections/39001121742996-Marathon-Error-Codes) While a decent amount showed up for the free weekend, it was very clear the servers did not. Constant disconnects prevented many sessions during that crucial launch period, especially for some of my friends who were cautious about the game but willing to give it a shot. Despite the increase in traffic over the end of Season 1, queue times had never felt longer. Eons passed staring at the loading screen, praying to Durandal it would even connect, much less stay connected throughout the game. The worst server issues were fixed, and I certainly won’t look several blue kits sideways as an apology, but it understandably frustrated a lot of people. Showing cracks like this when so much is against it is really unfortunate and I really wish Bungie could execute better on future launches.

For reasons partially deserved, others unfair, and many more understandable, *Marathon* has always struggled with its audience. It’s a challenging game with a competitive player-vs.-player slant in what’s already a niche, dwindling genre, facing a tougher and increasingly centralized live-service market. People’s understandable misgivings with *Destiny 2* (2017) carried over to this game, as this miasma of Bungie’s fallen reputation through plagiarism and content removal hung over the game. The subsequent disappointment of *D2*’s shutdown and Sony’s [extremely](https://www.theguardian.com/games/article/2024/sep/03/playstation-5-shooter-concord-taken-offline-just-two-weeks-after-release) [poor](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/action/cancelled-the-last-of-us-online-director-says-it-was-the-best-multiplayer-game-the-team-had-ever-played/) [history](https://youtu.be/ZMUJT64WDWI?si=wUUeH8ejMM77ddGK) with [recurring games](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/playstation-reboots-horizon-hunters-gathering-as-live-service-strategy-struggles?accessToken=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJzb3VyY2UiOiJTdWJzY3JpYmVyR2lmdGVkQXJ0aWNsZSIsImlhdCI6MTc4NzE0NjczMywiZXhwIjoxNzg3NzUxNTMzLCJhcnRpY2xlSWQiOiJUSzBOVElSS1YyVjUwMCIsImJjb25uZWN0SWQiOiJCMUVBQkI5NjQ2QUM0REZFQTJBRkI4MjI1MzgyQTJFQSJ9.bCZhw-Gvp753Tyh6Q5RMS_QZQKay0MHWPN7zc4zETyk&leadSource=article-gifting) certainly didn’t help either. Discourse over its player count remains its biggest point of general relevance, a pursuit I find deeply grim as a way people engage with this medium. Interested players expect the game’s death, and in a rebirth of *Titanfall*’s narrative design concepts, the same player response reanimated too.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/cradle.jpg", "A screenshot of *Marathon* demonstrating The Cradle: Direct energy to your WEAVEworm colonies to customize the stats of your shells. Increase your available energy by completing contracts or sacrificing items in your vault to the Matter Converter.", "One of my favorite new features in Season 2.", "[Bungie](https://press.bungie.com/Marathon#?tab=screenshots-1&scrollto=)." %}

There are great parts of Season 2, don’t get me wrong. The Cradle is a wonderful addition to progression and agency, offering a vastly more effective remedy to hoarding by allowing unused loot to directly boost your Shell’s stats instead. While a late Season 1 addition, the C.A.A.R.I CyAc Commendations smartly reward teamwork with other runners, adding an undercurrent of humanity with enemy interactions. The new weapons, the KKV-9SD and D54 Battle Pistol, are fun to use, and the continual balance changes (especially to throwables) have been mostly positive in my experience. While I’m committed to my main Vandal, Sentinel being a defensive Shell adds variety to team composition and heightens environmental awareness through tracker abilities. Finding myself in the same place I was months ago with an empty vault and a chip on my shoulder, I felt more capable than I ever was at the start of Season 1.

However, no server issue or minutes-long load time would ever fix the biggest disappointment when you actually could load into a match: Priority Contracts had not changed whatsoever between Seasons. Without the weight of every run counting towards discovering new things about the world and with matches taking forever to load into, I began to drift from the game. Since its opening weeks and despite the great changes, I’ve admittedly barely played much of Season 2. As a social experience, it’s a lonelier game to play than it used to be. Friends took Sponsored Survival, a Sponsored Kit PvE mode with Rooks, for a spin but mostly hopped ship when that experiment had passed. Night Marsh unfortunately never stuck with me either, its slower pace and isolation only contributing to being confounding and empty to navigate in my experience. With my interest dissipating and writer’s block striking, this essay was soon to follow suit, forever languishing in my already long writing backlog. I didn’t think I’d even publish it this far after I started. That was until Vault Breaker.

## Vault Breaker:…They Are Waiting.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/ship.png", "A screenshot of *Marathon* during the cutscene that introduces Cryo Archive. It shows a green 3D blueprint of the UESC Marathon ship.", null, "My screenshot of *Marathon*." %}

The 3 base maps of *Marathon*, Perimeter, Dire Marsh, and Outpost slot in pretty well as easy, medium, and hard, respectively. Each one gradually increases loot rarity, location density, environmental hazards, and map interactions. Perimeter is spread out with escarpments and tunnels that connect points of interest underground, allowing a more controlled pace. Dire Marsh introduces activities like Lockdowns and caches and places POIs opposite each other, making the open grasslands riskier to traverse due to easier sightlines and increased verticality. Outpost, the most difficult map, requires a search for keycards to access the UESC-flooded Pinwheel for higher-tier loot and forces close encounters with its Heat Cascade rain effect. While Dire Marsh’s bare visibility can be frustrating and Outpost’s keycard probability has landed my squad in trouble, I find all 3 zones balanced and conducive to *Marathon*’s gameplay loop.

Cryo Archive is often the most celebrated portion of *Marathon*, and for good reason: it takes the core map design principles to their furthest extreme while incorporating significant narrative relevance. While my time with the 1994 game was limited, a labyrinth on the UESC Marathon itself couldn’t help but evoke that game's turbulent, oppressive, and multifaceted map ethos. Aligning that with exemplary art direction and the objective design *NuMarathon* accomplishes so well is a match made in heaven. It’s just a shame I never got to play much of Cryo Archive in Season 1. As someone who played more and more solo as the season went on, by the time I could have the loot to play Cryo, I’d be unable to get a full squad of 3 to be able to explore it. The few attempts I did make, including on random-fill, went about as well as you could expect: an impossible factory of brutal bots, lost loot, and salivating sharks eager to camp you. As the developers plan to introduce more PVE to the game, an experimental mode dedicated to exploring the game’s biggest map (with solo play!) piqued my interest. After time away, I was back on Vandal, running for my life.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/haunts.png", "A screenshot of *Marathon* during the cutscene that introduces Cryo Archive. It shows a black 3D blueprint of the UESC Marathon ship cast on a green background.", null, "My screenshot of *Marathon*." %}

The goal of Vault Breaker is to complete objectives to access Vaults progressively further into Cryo Archive and upgrade a specialized Sponsor Kit, either by exfil or death by trying. Fortunately, death isn’t for nothing. Vault Breaker serves as a formal expansion of *Marathon*’s roguelite undercurrents by rewarding two-tiered Vault Data as its primary progression factor. Obtained by executing the UESC commanders (Tier 1) and uncovering Vaults and the Index items within (Tier 1/2), even failed runs can still pay off. Players can spend Vault Data to upgrade a mode-exclusive loadout for the next run or cash out with gear for your standard inventory from the Armory. The progression of Cryo Archive otherwise stays intact as a standard run. UESC and hacked Monitors drop Security Tags to increase your Security Clearance, expanding the areas you’re able to access, while found Batteries, Cryo Coolant, Credentials, and Keys unlock the Vaults. The player has agency in the success they revel in, and like Contracts, every run has real stakes while remaining continuously beneficial, taking great advantage of Cryo Archive’s design.

Truly a marathon to delve into, Cryo Archive’s inherent map design focuses on interactive tasks, environmental mystery, and ruthless difficulty fit PvE like a glove. The map remains fun to navigate on every run: new angles and sightlines appear from nowhere and make pushes against enemies constantly engaging. Vandal’s mobility really shines, perhaps more than any other zone in *Marathon*. However, Cryo’s massive size takes a lot of time to adjust to, and playing with a squad requires smart routing and teamwork. To some extent, a lack of player guidance is a structural matter: the compact, layered architecture leads to a shortage of identifiable landmarks to rely on for navigation. Luckily, Scanners do reveal POIs needed for finding items like Batteries and interactable Monitors typically signify the completion of encounters, but otherwise, I found myself lost many times. The lack of in-game explanation of the importance and function of Batteries, Coolant, and the items unique to Cryo was frustrating as a new player. With such a strict 30-minute timer only compounding on a lack of guidance, solo play was exceedingly difficult even compared to the solo Outpost runs I’m more accustomed to. Admittedly, I never felt true despair but did desire tuning to better accommodate those tackling Cryo alone. Thankfully, later updates addressed this directly via a dedicated queue featuring an elongated 40-minute timer, 2 minute final exfil timer, increased player damage and reduced UESC aggression. It’s a good testament to the devs’ (for the most part) continued ear to the ground that despite limited time, tangible improvements iterated, not corrupted, the mode’s appeal.

I didn’t entirely know how this mode would land when it originally got announced: I have limited experience with roguelites as is, and certainly a lack of contracts didn’t help my worries. At first blush, Vault Breaker lacks the focus on extraction elements, player encounters, and narrative structure that made Marathon so appealing to me to begin with. Despite my worries, I find Vault Breaker remarkably succeeds in its experimentation, recontextualizing the game’s core friction and progression for a more focused, approachable experience I deeply crave. The game remains as challenging and rewarding as it ever was and, in some runs, has ever been for me. I only successfully exfilled a handful of times in my many attempts, but the blue beam never felt too impossible for my group and I. Speaking of, my group! For a couple of late evenings, the gamers and I willingly sent ourselves to the death factory, laughing, stressing, and enjoying the whole way, just as we used to. Vault Breaker, not in the stead of *Marathon*’s core gameplay but in tandem with it, reminds me why I fell in love with this game to begin with. For that, I’m thankful and itchy for it to return, as I believe within Vault Breaker is a glimpse into what *Marathon* can become.

## The Future: Let’s all keep pushing forward. Momentum.

https://youtu.be/YaLvrG6-kkM?si=sm6i3S5y4zBfVkPI

As I look back at months-long notes while writing this, there’s a stark change in tune about *Marathon* and its future. What started as optimism turned skeptical, distant, worried, and hopeful again as time went on. Fitting that a game with so many ebbs and flows would embody it the whole way down, even if I halfheartedly joke the next Steam update may be the “one”. It’d be naive to ignore the drop in players, but [Sony’s affirmations on their support](https://www.sony.com/en/SonyInfo/IR/library/presen/er/pdf/25q4_sonyspeech.pdf) do bring me a little comfort, as much as their word can anyway. Every time I came back to this draft, more news came out and first impressions on them lengthened the writing process substantially. Last time I drafted, [layoffs refocused the studio on Marathon](https://sonyinteractive.com/en/news/blog/an-update-from-playstation-studios-2/), [game director Joe Ziegler departed the studio](https://x.com/Ziegler_Dev/status/2078162822559641797), and [a commitment to more PvE modes](https://www.bungie.net/7/en/News/Article/launch_learning_whatsnext) was formally made. Through the string of notes, Discord conversations, and scribbled drafts, one consistency is clear: I don’t know what the future holds for *Marathon*. Soberingly, Bungie seems to know. Accompanied by unvaulting Destiny 2’s campaigns, a call for accountability, delaying Season 3: Symbiosis to December and an October Nightfall Refresh was a promise and a plan to evolve Marathon. On September 24th, [The Future of Marathon](https://www.bungie.net/7/en/News/Article/futureofmarathon) (alongside a Behind the Scenes Dev Insights video) sets out to fulfill that promise in a frankly honest, realistic look at what’s next.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/refresh_roadmap.jpg", "A roadmap calendar of Marathon Season 2: Nightfall's refresh.", "The roadmap for the Nightfall Refresh.", "[Bungie](https://x.com/MarathonDevTeam/status/2100253598609535344)." %}

October will reset progress and inventories in Season 2: Nightfall as a 9 week prelude to Season 3, featuring several experiments to build feedback for Symbiosis’ permanent PvE mode. Vault Breaker (!!) returns for Week 3-5 with the currently active Sponsored Survival returning afterward: I’m looking forward to second opinions on these modes, especially the latter. Sponsored Queues, a Sponsored Kit-exclusive extraction mode, and Cryo Archive are active for the entire refresh as a good way to appeal to both newcomers and those seeking brutality, respectively. The great, player-friendly trends of C.A.R.R.I. and raising the gear pool floor by upgrading Sponsored Kits to Enhanced rarity near the end of the season continue. Unfortunately, Ranked is one of the few things not returning for the refresh, so I’ll have to have my first go at it in Season 3 instead. While disappointing given Symbiosis was delayed mere days before its expected reveal, having a second start and fresh look at Season 2 is something I’m oddly looking forward to.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/perimeter.jpg", "An in-development screenshot of the new Perimeter map in Season 3: Symbiosis. Red fungi surround a forest with two Shells walking through the treacherous, barren land. ", null, "[Bungie](https://www.bungie.net/7/en/News/Article/futureofmarathon)." %}

As for Season 3, Symbiosis’ delay to December 8th seems to attempt to relaunch the game as a multifaceted survival experience, headlined by the game’s first permanent PvE mode. While there’s unfortunately no concrete details, if it's anything like Vault Breaker, I will be extremely happy to spend my evenings tearing through the mode. A Team Deathmatch experiment sounds fun and Bishop, “built around speed, surprise, and devastating bursts of melee damage may be the one Shell to get me to stray from Vandal, at least to try. Besides PvE, though, the biggest addition is the massive revamp to Perimeter. While I like Perimeter as a zone, I found I missed a lot of the objectives, unique PoIs, and high loot rewards that other maps provided. This new redesign seems to do just that: the new map has a fungal tone, new PoIs and areas, and the gigantic Tick Queen which sounds awesome to play. Adding more objective-based progression to the earliest map as well as a socializing firing range can do wonders for onboarding players on what makes this game tick. Narratively, we’re still in the dark beyond “more focused questlines”, although the Pfhor from the original trilogy and more alien combatants, more alien direction in general, sounds so fascinating. Along with the other improvements, I can only dream Symbiosis’ Priority Contracts evolve this game’s science fiction, alongside whatever March 2027 brings in its attempt to interconnect PvE, PvP, and PvEvP. Given the circumstances and current sentiment, the multistage refocusing under new game director Del Chafe III and creative director Julia Nardin’s vision starting with Season 3 has me pretty excited.

{% image "/posts/2026/marathon/symbiosis.jpg", "A render for Marathon Season 3: Symbiosis featuring several Shells in the grass shoot the gigantic Tick Queen surrounded by purple rain and fire.", "*Marathon*'s renders continue to look incredible.", "[Bungie](https://press.bungie.com/Marathon#?tab=screenshots-1&scrollto=)." %}

In the wake of this refocusing, if I only had one request, one piece of feedback to Bungie, it's that Vault Breaker becomes a north star for *Marathon*, especially for PvE. As the game adapts to other experiences, I believe *Marathon* is best served in the design that was the very reason for that mode’s success. More engaging, contrasting maps with phenomenal art direction, environmental design, and activity-based progression as Cryo Archive is rightfully acclaimed for accomplishing. New ways to explore the starkly isolating, harsh reality of Tau Ceti IV that furnish the capacity to learn and emerge, whether through PvE, Team Deathmatch, or firing ranges. Maintaining meaningful value and structure to every run, through progression with Vault Data and the Cradle or narratively with new Priority Contracts and forms of interactive storytelling. Most of all, I wish that in whatever direction *Marathon* sprints into, the emergent gameplay that provides such tension and satisfaction in the struggle to survive is never lost. Execution certainly matters; Season 2’s shaky technical launch was as good of an example as any. But if first impressions mean anything (and they do), I’m incredibly excited for Season 3 and if done well, could do incredible things for the game that previous iterations couldn’t.

Even still though, looking through notes from the past few months of my life on this one game, I find myself not caring. I certainly have my preference in direction, but I’ve gotten so much from this game in ways no Steam description or new season or big relaunch could ever capture. For a game so isolating in gameplay and reception alike, I’ve gotten to truly connect with friends through the medium I love in a way I was rarely comfortable with before. I grew to accept losses in a way years of playing fighting games casually (usually because I’m too busy hosting for them) couldn’t instill in me. Though my luck with troubled multiplayer titles [doesn’t seem to be ending anytime soon](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/2xko-active-development-ends-december-2026), I’ve since returned to parries and blockstrings in no small thanks to *Marathon*. It opened my eyes to a genre I largely wasn’t as accepting of beforehand, let alone being an insightful, fun, and brilliantly designed experience on its own merits. Wherever *Marathon* ends up, cooperative or competitive, extraction or survival, thriving or departed, I’ll be there. Shells wash up on the ocean again in due time.
