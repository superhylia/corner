I’m not much of a fan of shooters. At least, I thought I wasn't. Growing up, shooters were “The Game.” After *Halo: Combat Evolved* (2001) shaped expectations for the genre on consoles, the massive critical and financial successes of both *Halo 3* (2007) and *Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare* (2007) set the dominance of the first-person shooter in motion. Just as the platformer had before it, the “machine” kept turning, slowly homogenizing a genre into one style, one audience, and one goal: money. Admittedly, it’s a harsh outlook to have, but fatigue with the genre set in very quickly, even at a young age, as series like *CoD*, its competitors and copycats alike, released every year without fail. I let the genre pass me by as a blur of car hums, grey skies, and brownish trees. The genre just wasn’t for me, and I didn’t think it ever would be.  

Fast forward a few years, and resistance to this stance started to grow. *Splatoon* (2015) came out and ran counter to every expectation I had for the genre: it was Nintendo, it was weird, and for god’s sake, it was colorful. I don’t play as much as I used to, but *Splatoon 2* (2017) got quite a few hours out of me, with *Octo Expansion* (2018) piquing my interest when I get the time. *Metroid Prime* (2002), while not having a strong combat focus, blew the doors off what a shooter *could* be and remains one of the finest-designed games ever put on disc. Lost in the blur of ignoring the genre were great campaigns I nervously dipped my toe into. My first experiences with the *Halo* trilogy, *Vanquish* (2010), and especially *Titanfall 2* (2016) made them some of my favorite games of all time, with packed pacing, movement mechanics, and great gunplay. There were shooters I liked now\!

When *Marathon* (2026) was first revealed, I knew very little about the series' lineage beyond its status as an [Apple Pippin oddity](https://breezewiki.com/pippin/wiki/Super_Marathon) (I don't know how I'm like this either) and Mac classic. Given Sony’s track record with multiplayer shooters, I, like many others, did not have high hopes for the game. Expectations certainly weren’t helped by Bungie’s continued mismanagement, tarnished reputation, and now even plagiarism, marring the game's nascent reception. It would probably pass me by again as yet another multiplayer title for someone primarily into crafted solo experiences. But something about it stuck out to me…  

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXhQbF7TlbA  

It had art direction. A strange, strange world of danger, organically robotic. The colors were so stark, almost plastic in their sheen. It was striking. In a games industry that’s consistently prioritized graphical fidelity above all else, *Marathon* had real character to its look. A friend of mine, deeply in love with Bungie’s past works, encouraged our group to try the game out during the Server Slam. Outside of fighting games, *Among Us* (2018), and the occasional *Monster Hunter* *Rise* (2021) session, I rarely ever played games online with others, competitively or cooperatively. Fears of my inability to play well or contribute to my team kept me at a distance from the genre, so I only tackled it alone. My friends pushed me, though, and having nothing to lose, I gave it an honest shot.

## Gameplay: Death is the First Step

As one of eight unique Shell characters in *Marathon*, the player explores the item-laden, hazardous maps of extrasolar colony Tau Ceti IV against other players and UESC enemies (PvPvE) in 25-minute runs. Runners risk time and inventory to uncover gear for empowered combat, complete narrative Contract missions for capability-enhancing factions, and reach an ‘exfil’ extraction point to keep found spoils. As someone inexperienced with online shooters, the extraction shooter genre, and competitive multiplayer games as a whole, I found *Marathon* entrancing in the most alien ways. *Marathon* is oppressive, flowing, tactical. Bungie’s gunplay continues to be the best in the industry: every weapon and throwable has its own kinesthetic identity that goes beyond abilities and range. Machine guns feel heavy, shotguns feel nimble, and the Hardline PR feels like a dogshit weapon to use without upgrades, every bullet choking out of its barrel. Tau Ceti IV is ruthless, bordering on survival horror in solo play. Every shot ringing out behind you could be a bot, a turret, or a player, and with time-to-kill so quick in this game, one wrong move truly can be the end of a run.

Within that ruthlessness comes *Marathon*’s greatest strength: its emergent gameplay. Emergent gameplay has quite a [few](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_gameplay) [different](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/examining-emergent-gameplay) [definitions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jWEuSV_nbfA), but I feel a remarkable moment from one of my favorite games ever made shows it to you.  

[https://youtu.be/9SNrFaXCm1E](https://youtu.be/9SNrFaXCm1E)  

In *The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom* (2023), Link has access to 5 Zonai abilities that allow him to terraform and interact with the environment around him. Typically, when fighting the recurring Flux Guardians, I use the same surefire strategy of bomb arrows to sink it closer to the ground and then Ascend through it to hit its core weak point, rinse and repeat. On this attempt, however, I accidentally use Rewind during the minibosses' spread attack (you can see my confusion by attempting both Ascend and Rewind again) and realize: *I can ride the blocks up.* This moment exemplifies both how much I love the *Breath of the Wild* duology and what makes *Marathon* shine: the interaction of separate mechanics that, dev-intended or not, creates complex outcomes and creative strategies.

The nature of extraction shooters is a volatile dance between risk and reward. In fear of loss, I fell back on Sponsored Kits from the armory. What seems like a low-risk, high-reward option becomes a crucial lesson in adaptability and comfort, a vestige of *Zelda*’s weapon durability that I defend, if not at least understand. Relying on a basic loadout of low-end weapons and unextractable consumables puts the player at a disadvantage compared to those who risk better loadouts. Bring little to nothing into a run? A few shots is all it’ll take to send you back to the lobby. Carry too much? Run out of inventory for even better scraps of material. Hoard your loot? Vault space can fill much faster than upgrades will expand it, forcing you to chance the stuff you’ve meticulously collected in the next run and maybe even leave some of it behind. Push on a trio or take on a Warden? The rich may only get richer with your signature on the invoice in blue blood. Come in loaded, stay longer, and go farther on the toughest maps for the best of the best pickups, but face one bad encounter and gamble everything you’ve found and everything you’ve started with. Dependency becomes futile.

The brilliance of *Marathon* is that all of this emergence, born from risk and reward, extends to every stage of the game for every skill level. As you get further into the match, your affordances grow: there are fewer players who are rotating the map, meaning points of interest (POI) are now within your grasp that weren’t at the beginning. If you can stick the run out, coming in with better gear can heavily tip the scales in early engagement. Special keys unlock hidden areas or lockbox chests for your troubles. Coordination with your squad and maximizing each Shell’s abilities mix into a concoction of flanking and pressure that can pay out big. Decisions become playbooks, pressure becomes confidence, and the environment around you becomes a tool in your arsenal. UESC aren’t only foes, but friends if their attention is on another group: who's to say you can’t cause that? The player becomes familiar with the rotating weapons provided by the sponsored kits, how they’ll execute, and grows their strategy from there. Eventually, the shell takes its first flight away from the nest, going further and further into the wild with every take-off to forage and collect.

## Progression: Expect Death. Embrace Rebirth.

After delivering a once-in-a-generation title in *Call of Duty 4*: *Modern Warfare* (2007), Infinity Ward’s founders Jason West and the late Vince Zampella wanted more creative control and bonuses from their contracts. Activision shakily agreed on the condition that they would develop *Modern Warfare 2* (2009) and control would revert if the duo ever left, from what I’m sure Bobby Kotick called “unforeseen circumstances.” A few lawsuits over wrongful termination, an exodus of 46 developers, and a deal with Electronic Arts later, the two formed Respawn Entertainment and produced *Titanfall* (2013). Highly anticipated, *Titanfall*, as Zampella put it, brought “scale, verticality, and story” to the genre with a unique proposition: what if the campaign *was* the multiplayer? Players would experience a nine-mission campaign across two team-based game types through interstitial dialogue, two factions, and specialized map variants featuring scripted setpieces like dragons and escape sequences.

The multiplayer campaign didn’t receive the greatest reception. Reviews found it shallow, dull, and poorly voiced, no more than background noise to what was otherwise a frenetic and quality shooter. Despite a big launch, players fell off the game, while [curious buyers were averse](https://www.polygon.com/2016/8/18/12533412/titanfall-2-single-player-demo-gamescom-2016/) to a full-price package without a complete solo mode to fall back on. This feedback eventually led to *Titanfall 2*’s celebrated campaign, marrying Titan setpieces with terrific traversal, phenomenal puzzle-solving, and [legendary level design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effect_and_Cause). A thousand miles away and a generation later, Bungie found themselves asking the same questions Respawn did. How do you weave a harrowing sci-fi scare of corporatized mercenary research and failed expeditions in a way that leaves an effect on matches and players?

Contracts and the Codex were their answer, and, in my eyes, *Marathon*’s biggest accomplishment. Offered by one of six Factions, Contracts bestow loot, reputation for Faction upgrade trees, and one-time Priority Contracts that serve as narrative vignettes into Tau Ceti IV. Boosts to vitals, enhancements to abilities, and access to items are offered by the mercy of Monsanto-like mega corporations, death cultists, radical terrorists, and even the makers of the Shells themselves. From defeating a set of enemies with specific weapons to carrying and installing implants back and forth across the map, these objectives range in zone and condition. All of this then feeds into the Codex, which rewards you with free cosmetics, occasional loot, and lore entries to read and listen to that further develop the world. Everything serves to benefit, and for someone new to the genre, these became my foundation. Consistent, variable objectives that advance the narrative and progression on every run, whether successful or failed, gave me room to push further. Early exits stung, but never scarred. Exfils felt more rewarding than just “surviving” or even “winning,” but experiencing. It was progress.  

Cast into an ever-changing shadow of a body destined for killing, there’s a strange illumination that still shines on the story that only a Shell could provide. When thinking back on one of my favorite Contracts, I realize that very little of each of *Marathon*’s zones is occupied by anything resembling a human: there are bots, there are plants, but no standing life to occupy it. The times something stands, it's another player governed by the whims of whatever feeds their hunger best; evidently, I became very enticing to Shell and Scout alike. When I went to complete NuCaloric’s Paradigm Shift 1/5, I scanned samples in low tides across the east side of Perimeter as trigger-happy UESC nipped at my heels, eager to drown me. The music tensed as I snuck through the tunnels as other players stamped to gain riches above and behind me. Against the clock, having to cover so much distance, my Vandal rushed to the East Wall only to find something that resembled…me? It spoke. “This is Dr. Song, dying far from home. I did it. I saw you. Named you. Dragged you into the light. Telomyces mosaica. That's a fitting name for an endlessly mutating killer.”  

[https://www.marathonwiki.com/Lore:Clarissa\_Song](https://www.marathonwiki.com/Lore:Clarissa_Song) image  

Endlessly mutating killer is fitting for what I became. I died a lot. Lost a lot of good stuff. A Hardline PR can send you back to homelessness very quickly. Eventually, Perimeter felt like my world to conquer. I learned that Disrupt Cannon can push enemies away from cover at the right angle. Footsteps became my metronome as the bushes of Dire Marsh became my home. I started playing solo, and the fear of letting my trio down no longer felt as malignant when I was playing more than them. Greed comes for everyone, and “just one more key” in Outpost cleared a lot of my inventory. Like an animal, I went back to hunt. To flank, to push, to hide, and to run: these are the lessons of the wilderness that became installed into me, a Shell of myself, one that itched and scratched for just one more run. As I closed the game for the night, I winced at my Steam hours, not used to seeing triple digits in only 2 months. The button still hovered over Play. Maybe I was a fan of shooters after all. 

## Reception and Season 2: Somewhere in the Heavens…

This essay took me a long time to write, drafting my thoughts on the game during the pre-launch playtests. 100+ hours later, this game has sucked me into its gravitational pull in a way very few games in recent memory have. As my friends gradually ramped on and off the game, I kept extracting almost every day. Just one more run before bed. One more run til the end of this podcast. One more run. The fear of futility to my team and to my improvement had finally washed away. While worries about the game's future have always been an unfortunate downer to this game's reputation, things looked up for *Marathon*. Season 2: Nightfall brought a brand-new map in Night Marsh, the Sentinel Shell, major improvements like the Cradle, Sponsored Survival for PVP-lite, and a free weekend to get billions on the game. A firesale of all of my loot in some of the most fun matches I’ve had with the game as the clock ticked closer to the reset. I couldn’t have been more excited for June 2nd.

The day came. [So did the jungle animals.](https://help.marathonthegame.com/hc/en-us/sections/39001121742996-Marathon-Error-Codes) While a decent amount showed up for the free weekend, it was very clear the servers did not. Constant disconnects prevented many sessions during that crucial onboarding period, especially for some of my friends who were cautious about the game but willing to give it a shot. Despite the increase in traffic over the end of Season 1, queue times had never felt longer. Eons passed staring at the loading screen, praying to Durandal it would even connect, much less stay connected throughout the game. The worst server issues were fixed, and I certainly won’t look several blue kits sideways as an apology, but it understandably frustrated a lot of people. Showing cracks like this when so much is against it is really unfortunate, and while I’m sure layoffs play some part, I really wish Bungie can execute better on future launches.

For reasons partially deserved, others unfair, and many more understandable, *Marathon* has always struggled with its audience. It’s a challenging game with a competitive player-vs.-player slant in what’s already a niche, dwindling genre, facing a tougher and increasingly centralized live-service market. People’s understandable misgivings with *Destiny 2* (2017) carried over to this game, as this miasma of Bungie’s fallen reputation through plagiarism and content removal hung over the game. The subsequent disappointment of *D2*’s shutdown and Sony’s [extremely](https://www.theguardian.com/games/article/2024/sep/03/playstation-5-shooter-concord-taken-offline-just-two-weeks-after-release) [poor](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/action/cancelled-the-last-of-us-online-director-says-it-was-the-best-multiplayer-game-the-team-had-ever-played/) [history](https://youtu.be/ZMUJT64WDWI?si=wUUeH8ejMM77ddGK) with [recurring games](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/playstation-reboots-horizon-hunters-gathering-as-live-service-strategy-struggles?accessToken=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJzb3VyY2UiOiJTdWJzY3JpYmVyR2lmdGVkQXJ0aWNsZSIsImlhdCI6MTc4NzE0NjczMywiZXhwIjoxNzg3NzUxNTMzLCJhcnRpY2xlSWQiOiJUSzBOVElSS1YyVjUwMCIsImJjb25uZWN0SWQiOiJCMUVBQkI5NjQ2QUM0REZFQTJBRkI4MjI1MzgyQTJFQSJ9.bCZhw-Gvp753Tyh6Q5RMS_QZQKay0MHWPN7zc4zETyk&leadSource=article-gifting) certainly didn’t help either. Discourse over its player count remains its biggest point of general relevance, a pursuit I find deeply grim as a way people engage with this medium. Interested players expect the game’s death, and in a rebirth of *Titanfall*’s narrative design concepts, the same player response reanimated too.

There were good parts of Season 2, don’t get me wrong. The Cradle is a wonderful addition to progression, being a more effective remedy to loot hoarding and indecision by allowing unused loot to directly boost your Shell’s stats. While a late Season 1 addition, the C.A.A.R.I CyAc Commendations smartly reward teamwork with other runners, adding an undercurrent of humanity with enemy interactions. The new weapons in the KKV-9SD and D54 Battle Pistol are fun to use and the continual balance changes (especially to throwables) have been mostly positive in my experience. While I’m committed to my main Vandal, Sentinel being a defensive Shell adds variety to team composition and heightens environmental awareness through tracker abilities in a neat way. Finding myself at the same place I was months ago with an empty vault and a chip on my shoulder, I felt more capable in an environment more facilitating of growth.

No server issue or minutes-long load time would ever fix the biggest disappointment when you actually could load into a match: Priority Contracts had not changed whatsoever between Seasons. Without the weight of every run counting towards discovering new things about the world and with matches taking forever to load into, I began to drift from the game. I’ve barely played much of Season 2 in regular matches since its opening weeks despite all the good changes to be quite honest. It’s a lonelier game to play than it used to be: friends took Sponsored Survival for a spin but mostly hopped ship when that experiment had passed. Night Marsh unfortunately never stuck with me either, its slower pace and isolation only contributing to being confounding and empty to navigate. With my interest dissipating, this essay was soon to follow suit, forever languishing in my already long writing backlog or maybe not seeing the light of day at all. That was until Vault Breaker.

## Vault Breaker:…They Are Waiting. 

The 3 base maps of *Marathon*, Perimeter, Dire Marsh, and Outpost slot in pretty well to easy, medium, and hard, respectively. Each one gradually increases loot rarity, location density, environmental hazards, and map interactions. Perimeter is spread out with escarpments and tunnels that connect POIs underground, allowing a more controlled pace. Dire Marsh introduces activities like Lockdowns and caches and places POIs opposite each other, making the open grasslands riskier to traverse due to easier sightlines and increased verticality. Outpost, the most difficult map, requires a search for keycards to access the UESC-flooded Pinwheel for higher-tier loot and forces close encounters with its Heat Cascade rain effect. While Dire Marsh’s sightlines can be frustrating and Outpost’s keycard probability has landed my squad in trouble, I find all 3 to be balanced and conducive to *Marathon*’s gameplay loop.

Cryo Archive is often the most celebrated portion of *Marathon*, and for good reason: it takes the core map design principles to their furthest extreme while incorporating significant narrative relevance. While my time with the 1994 game was limited, a labyrinth on the UESC Marathon itself couldn’t help but evoke that game's turbulent, oppressive, and multifaceted map ethos. Aligning that with exemplary art direction and the objective design *NuMarathon* accomplishes so well is a match made in heaven. It’s just a shame I never got to play much of Cryo Archive in Season 1. As someone who played more and more solo as the season went on, by the time I could have the loot to play Cryo, I’d be unable to get a full squad of 3 to be able to explore it. The few attempts I did make, including on random-fill, went about as well as you could expect: an impossible factory of brutal bots, lost loot, and salivating sharks eager to camp you. As the developers plan to introduce more PVE to the game, a full mode dedicated to exploring it with solo play piqued my interest. After time away, I was back on Vandal, running for my life.

## The Future: 
I don’t know what the future holds for *Marathon*. [More layoffs refocusing all of Bungie on *Marathon*](https://sonyinteractive.com/en/news/blog/an-update-from-playstation-studios-2/), [game director Joe Ziegler’s departure](https://x.com/Ziegler_Dev/status/2078162822559641797), and [a commitment to more PvE modes](https://www.bungie.net/7/en/News/Article/launch_learning_whatsnext) all leave *Marathon* on shakier ground. It’d be naive to ignore the drop in players, but Sony’s affirmations on their support does bring me a little comfort, as much as their word can anyway. Here’s to hoping Season 3 will reintroduce unique Priority Contracts and that its PvE modes make as much of a splash as Vault Breaker did for me. Even still though, looking through notes from the past few months of my life on this one game, I find myself not caring. For a game so isolating in gameplay and reception alike, I’ve gotten to truly connect with friends through the medium I love in a way I was rarely comfortable with before. I grew to accept losses in a way years of playing fighting games casually (usually because I’m too busy hosting for them) couldn’t instill in me. I’ve since returned to parries and blockstrings in no small thanks to *Marathon*, though my luck with troubled multiplayer titles [doesn’t seem to be ending anytime soon](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/2xko-active-development-ends-december-2026). It opened my eyes to a genre I largely wasn’t as accepting of beforehand. Wherever *Marathon* ends up, thriving or departed, I’ll be there. Shells wash up on the ocean again with time.
