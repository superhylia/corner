## The Golden Age of Arcades: Striking Gold 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/space_invaders_hawaii.jpg", "Many people in Hawaii play *Space Invaders* (1978) arcade cabinets.", null, "Ethan Johnson of Play History" %}

The year is 1980. Times are good. Video games as a medium are in their golden age in the arcades, in no small part thanks to Japanese Taito Corporation designer Tomohiro Nishikado’s seminal shoot-em-up *Space Invaders* (1978)[^GS]. Both limited and enhanced by the public places coin-ops occupied, replayability as short-form experiences, the perusal of high scores, and the mantra of “easy to learn, hard to master” became their defining traits. Perception of mastery through leaderboards sparked competitive motivation in people to keep pumping quarters, and as these traits diversified into new genres like maze games in *Pac-Man* (1980), the arcades became ingrained in pop culture for decades to come. Success punted down to the home as the second generation of video game systems kicked off, and it was American video game corporation Atari’s time to dominate. As Taito’s blockbuster hit made waves as the first major arcade game to be ported to a home console, the Atari 2600, exclusive arcade conversions became the playbook that trampled the rest of the field, while third parties like Activision rose in response[^GS], leading to the game business we have now. It was a gold rush, and *everyone* wanted in, including a little hanafuda company from across the Pacific[^TR]. 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/hanafuda.png", "A game of Koi Koi being played with hanafuda cards made by Nintendo.", "Nintendo's hanafuda cards, aging back to possibly the Edo period in Japan.", "Marcus Richert." %}

Nintendo wouldn’t be as lucky. While their Game & Watch series of handheld LCD consoles, designed by Gunpei Yokoi[^ESC], would be their first success in electronic games, the failure to sell 3,000 machines of *Galaxian* (1979)-inspired *Radar Scope* (1980) in early 1981 left their American division hurting[^GB1]. Minoru Arakawa, head of Nintendo of America, begged his father-in-law and Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi to make a miracle. Supervised by Yokoi, it fell on one junior artist to convert the unused machines into something with worldwide appeal: Shigeru Miyamoto. With a storyline inspired by *King Kong* (1933) and *Beauty & the Beast*, the game evolved from a licensed *Popeye* game[^IGN] into one focused on vertical platforming as the hero Mario saves the Lady Pauline from captivity by his pet gorilla. It was a risky gamble, being so different from the maze and shooter titles that brought in the big bucks at the time, but Yamauchi had faith.[^GM] On what to call it, Miyamoto thought to title it after the strongest character, whom he described as a “stupid ape.” “Donkey Kong” would do just fine.

## Playing Donkey Kong: How high can you get? 

You probably know the rest. *Donkey Kong* becomes a breakthrough hit in 1981, iconizes gaming’s most famous character, establishes perhaps its most revered designer, and places Nintendo on the map.  Where the name came from or how much Ikegami Tsushinki was involved as a programming subcontractor[^GM] depends on who tells the story, but it’s a pretty good yarn to spin, one befitting a game so far ahead of its contemporaries that the player gets to witness the story unfold on screen. The girder beams of a construction site crumble as Donkey Kong stomps upon them, carrying Pauline to the top. Accompanied by music at the poles of the game, a few animations of Pauline’s cries for “HELP!,” hearts breaking, Donkey Kong smiling as Mario falters, and the ape’s dizzied defeat when Mario succeeds are all the game has to convey its narrative. While quaint today, there’s a charm to a story being distilled in its most primitive form.

‘Primitive’ is a pretty apt descriptor for *Donkey Kong*, both the game and the character, though not disparagingly. Across the four levels, each another 25 meters of the Construction Site Donkey Kong climbs atop of, you jump (a first for a platforming game) and climb ladders as Mario to reach Pauline at the top within the bonus timer, gaining extra points by collecting Pauline’s three dropped items, avoiding, and with the Hammer, destroying stage hazards like Barrels and Fireballs. 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/25m.png", "A screenshot of the arcade version of Donkey Kong on the first level 25m. Mario is using a hammer to destroy barrels.", "25m or the Beam scene.", "PixelatedArcade." %}

25m is a good introductory stage, featuring Barrels for Mario to jump over or destroy using the Hammer as they either roll down the girders or ladders. As the game's only featured power-up, the Hammer is a fun but limiting ability. By restricting your vertical mobility by preventing Mario from jumping or climbing ladders, as well as having the potential to whiff hazards without the proper precision, the item is mostly situational when lots of Barrels or Fireballs are directly in front of or behind Mario. Barrels are enjoyable hazards to dodge and jump over for points, though the chance of them coming down ladders seems completely inconsistent, which was frustrating during my initial attempts. Mario’s control is very stiff, and while this does sprinkle in a bit of satisfaction for proper timing, appropriate for an arcade game, jumping across multiple Barrels can feel awkward and unresponsive. 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/50m.png", "A screenshot of the arcade version of Donkey Kong on the second level 50m. Mario stands near a hammer as the pies (or cement?) scroll across the screen near the burning oil barrel to be destroyed.", "50m or the Cement Factory scene.", "PixelatedArcade." %}

50m ups the difficulty very well, making the level design itself the hazard with retracting ladders and conveyor belts directing Mario into the cement tubs or “pies.” Fireballs, a rare hazard in 25m, are more prominent in this stage and follow Mario after emerging from the oil drums. The conveyors affecting Mario’s mobility, the need for timing with the retractable ladders while Fireballs nip at his heels, and Pauline’s three lost items, the Bag, the Hat, and the Parasol, rewarding lots of extra points, encourage the player to have more spatial awareness for their path up the stage. Completing this stage in a timely fashion is made more rewarding by the forward-thinking Bonus Timer. Future video games like *Super Mario Bros.: The Lost Levels* (1986) or *Sonic the Hedgehog* (1991) would include end-level score bonuses as a reward for mastering the level layouts, and *Donkey Kong* implements this speedrun appeal to great effect, even this early on in the platforming genre. The Bonus Timer imposes a constant life-loss risk if the player doesn’t hurry and actively show the fruit of their labor, with the listed points as the reward for completing the stage. 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/75m.png", "A screenshot of the arcade version of Donkey Kong on the third level 75m. Mario stands on a elevator near a Fireball climbing a nearby ladder.", "75m or the Elevator scene.", "PixelatedArcade." %}

Despite being the second-most recognizable stage the game has to offer, 75m is unfortunately quite linear and my least favorite level. The stage builds on the conveyor belts concept, adding vertical movement via two elevators near the start of the stage, which do require some timing from the player. The Pauline item placement does add some difficulty, as the Hat requires Mario to crawl down the ladder before the second elevator, and the Purse requires Mario to be careful since the falling Jack spring’s throwing arc is very close to the edge of the platform. However, the elevators aren’t combined to any good use with the two Fireballs present in isolated locations in the remainder of the quite easy level, as the player only has the Jack to watch for. The lower part of the level is never necessary to go through, as the jump between the elevator and the floating platform isn’t particularly difficult to make. 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/100m.png", "A screenshot of the arcade version of Donkey Kong on the fourth and final level 100m. Mario is standing near a hammer while gaining 100 points for unscrewing the third of eight total rivets needed to complete the level.", "100m or the Rivet scene.", "PixelatedArcade." %}

Thankfully, the game ends strongly with my favorite stage: 100m. The satisfaction the Pauline items provide is encouraging exploration and spatial awareness for the entire level, rather than just what’s within Mario’s peripheral range. The bolts tying the girders together implement that concept within the stage itself, as the player will need to find a good path to unscrew all eight bolts across four layers by walking or jumping over them rather than simply reaching the top. The Pauline items are in their trickiest placements yet, as Mario will likely be surrounded by up to five Firefoxes, faster Fireballs that chase Mario across the entire stage. By unscrewing the bolts, they’ll be restricted to the segment of the stage before the gap left by the bolt. Despite Firefoxes running away from the player like the ghosts in *Pac-Man* when used, the Hammer is most useful in 100m, since the stage's criss-crossing progression means Mario is more likely to actually be surrounded. Once all eight bolts are undone, Donkey Kong falls to his defeat headfirst as Mario rescues Pauline.

As is typical of arcade games, the game loops back to 25m, with difficulty and bonus points increasing for up to 5 distinct cycles, though to my surprise, this is the biggest difference in overseas localization. For this retrospective, I played both the final Japanese revision and the International version via Hamster's *Arcade Archives DONKEY KONG* (2018), the first official rerelease of the game's arcade version. While in the initial release, 25m, 50m, 75m, and 100m are accessible for all cycles, the International set gradually increases the stage count with each loop, only featuring all four levels in the third loop and a unique repeating sequence of up to six stages in future runs[^TCRF]. Presumably implemented to increase difficulty and replayability for Western players, I personally prefer the Japanese set for narrative and mechanical continuity during an initial runthrough, though I do believe the International set’s modulating series of stages for cycles four and beyond succeeds at adding challenge and makes for my favorite attempt at the arcade version.

| ---- | ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| 1 | 25m, 50m, 75m, 100m | 25m, 100m |
| 2 | 25m, 50m, 75m, 100m | 25m, 75m, 100m |

As a very rough draft for what the platforming genre would become, *Donkey Kong* certainly shows its 45 years of age, with cracks in its mobility and level design. However, the combination of speedrun appeal, verticality, and collectible placement proves that even this early on, these traits that I find so essential in one of my favorite genres are still enjoyable. The game remains a remarkable achievement, one worth the 30-minute-or-so exhibit in my eyes. For a retrospective series looking into some of Nintendo’s oldest titles as they originally stood in all their 8-bit charm and chide alike, it begins with the replication of one of gaming’s greatest. 

## Developing the Family Computer: Taking It Home

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/colecovision.jpg", "The box art for the ColecoVision version of Donkey Kong split across two pages. The left/back page has three screenshots demonstrating the games graphics and the three levels available. The right/front page shows the main key art for Donkey Kong by Nintendo with the claim that this version is \'JUST LIKE THE REAL ARCADE GAME\'. The key art is noticably in a more jungle-like environment than the art used for the arcade version.", "The box art for the ColecoVision version of Donkey Kong. Notice the screenshots on the backpage.", "Scan of box art by GrumbleDuke." %}

Eric Bromley is about to take a shit. Lead engineer on the ColecoVision, the second-generation console by future Cabbage Patch toy company Coleco, he and CEO Arnold Greenberg were on the same page: deliver arcade-quality cartridge-based video games to the home. Following in the footsteps of Atari’s successful arcade conversion strategy, he visited Nintendo to secure the rights to their slew of arcade games with mixed prospects. Tense negotiations with Hiroshi Yamauchi weren’t leading to the golden ticket, but Bromley enjoyed the late lunch meal at the end of the day. Excusing himself to the bathroom, he happened to stumble across a certain crimson cabinet on his way there. Unknown to the west and, more importantly, unknown to Atari, was *Donkey Kong*. $200,000 and a rights mix-up scare later, *Donkey Kong* in its home console debut would take the ColecoVision to a million units sold as its pack-in game.[^CV] An impressive port for its time, it wouldn’t be just Coleco with such ambition. Ideas sparked across continents, including for one Masayuki Uemara.

Uemara-san, head of Research & Development Department 2 (R&D2), saw staff flock to Yokoi’s R&D1 in droves to work on the Game & Watch, leaving a skeleton crew remaining with little to do. Despite the internal interest, however, President Yamauchi-san knew the monochrome machines that gave Nintendo its competitive edge wouldn’t last forever. Already late to the cartridge-based home console market, Uemara-san and R&D2 were tasked with Project GAMECOM, a codename for what would be Nintendo’s next home system:[^GB1]

1. It had to be out by mid-1982.[^IW]  
2. It had to cost around ¥4-5,000 to produce.[^SHM] 
3. It had to be powerful enough to have no competitors for three years.[^IW]  
4. It couldn’t be made by Sharp, the manufacturer of the Game & Watch.[^IW]

It was an impossible goal. Nintendo’s rock-bottom position on the waiting list for processors during a boom in personal computers in Japan meant they had no recourse to produce their own processors or to contact major manufacturers willing to take a risk on a nascent industry. Ricoh, a semiconductor factory desperately needing a boost in its operating rate, just so happened to call in.[^US]

> *“I wanted to check out every possibility, so I paid the factory a visit, and it just so happened that the person who had taught Nintendo the circuit design for Nintendo's video games a while back for TV Game 6 and TV Game 15 was there.* [Released in 1977 by Nintendo, both were first-generation home game systems containing 6 and 15 pong/tennis games, respectively.] *He was like, "Let's do it!" However, without an idea of what kind of console we wanted to make, without something to aim for, we couldn't move forward, so I asked-thinking he would probably say no-if he could make something that could run Donkey Kong.”* - Masayuki Uemara [^IW2]

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/prototype.jpg", "A prototype for the Nintendo Family Computer with two photos. The former shows the central processing unit module, while the latter shows the graphics/image processing unit module.", "A Famicom prototype, demonstrating the CPU and GPU.", "GlitterBerri." %}
{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/dkg&w_dpad.jpg", "A manual diagram demonstrating the controls and functionality of the Multi-Screen Game & Watch version of Donkey Kong in order to demonstrate the first implementation of the directional pad.", "The dual-screen adaptation of *Donkey Kong* for the Game & Watch was the first use of the D-pad in gaming history.", "Heidi/stopXwhispering from Retro Video Gaming." %}
{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/uemura.png", "A photo of lead project architect Masayuki Uemara-san playing the Family Computer game Tennis on a CRT television.", "Uemara-san playing *Tennis* (1985) on the finailzed retail Family Computer.", "Getty." %}

Hungry for a challenge, the two groups of engineers worked together. They took the D-Pad from the Game & Watch iteration of *Donkey Kong* for the controllers and attached the two included to the system to save cost.[^GB3] It had no keyboard and couldn’t be called a PC, but it also wasn’t a toy either. Uemara-san’s wife thought, “Perhaps you could say that it’s a family computer.”[^IAE] The name stuck. Launching in Japan on July 15th, 1983, Nintendo released the Family Computer, “Famicom” for short, for ¥14,800 and three titles at launch: *Popeye*, *Donkey Kong Jr.*, and *Donkey Kong*.

## The Famicom Version: Closer Than It Appears

> [!IMPORTANT] A note about the console name.
> The article will refer to the system as the **“Famicom”** or **“Family Computer”** rather than the “Nintendo Entertainment System” or “NES” for historical and contextual accuracy, as the system had not been released overseas at the time. 

In the introduction to this retrospective series, I questioned whether classic games and their appeal could erode into irrelevance or be replaced by remakes. When we look back on history, we sometimes act like we know it all, like we can see all of the puzzle pieces, and the solution is so obvious. You expect more because you’ve seen better, as unfair as that can be sometimes. As playing games in their ideal form becomes more of a commodity, with fan efforts in emulation and decompilation alongside quality official re-releases, I sometimes find those thoughts scribbled in my journal when playing something old. Admittedly, *Donkey Kong* for the Famicom started out as one of those puzzles for me. 

{% image "/posts/2026/ngp-famicom/dk/comparison.png", "A comparison between Donkey Kong (1983, Famicom) on the left and Donkey Kong (1981, arcade) on the right.", "Famicom version on the left, arcade version on the right." , "Famicom: me. Arcade: PixelatedArcade." %}

The Famicom version is still as enjoyable as it was in the arcades. For better or worse, the controls are recreated very faithfully, and despite the technical limitations of one of the system's initial releases, the graphics converted nicely as well. After so many attempts in the original, I did enjoy Game B, which introduces the greater difficulty of later loops conveniently available from the start, and the rearrangement of Lady’s items mixed things up as well. Relative to the arcade version, however, *Donkey Kong* (1983, Famicom) is a famously incomplete conversion, missing the entire 50m level, most animations, and all but the ending cutscene. The limitations of the Famicom led to a stretched aspect ratio and reduced color, causing the ape’s hand to overlap the color of his chest, making it unviewable.[^NCE] While the Game B mode is a nicety, it and an alternating multiplayer feature, in which players swap control of Mario after he completes a stage or loses a life, are the only additions the port makes. Coming right off the arcade version, I was initially puzzled by my thoughts on the port and how it differentiated the Family Computer from its competitors or the arcade games of that era at the system's launch. Without any original titles the Family Computer could call its own, *Donkey Kong*, being the only one of the three arcade conversions to lack significant content, admittedly soured me on the game a little bit. Nearly 3 decades later, however, the game would get a second chance.

Preloaded with Wii bundles to celebrate the 25th anniversary of the *Super Mario Bros.* series, *Donkey Kong Original Edition* (2010) is an enhanced revision of the Famicom version that repairs its biggest flaw: parity. 50m has been faithfully restored as a level, with Donkey Kong’s climbing animation now marking the transition between levels. As more of a ROM hack than a ground-up second port, even featuring real hardware compatibility, the animation and color variations are still confined by the Famicom version's standards. However, as an oddity I knew little about before researching this series, *Original Edition* is a rare example of an older game being given a second chance to be made whole again. Given that some may prefer the 4:3 presentation of the 1983 game, I’d recommend it as the definitive version of the Famicom game…if it were made available again.

Availability of a game’s ideal form is, unfortunately, a privilege rather than a right. In an industry that prioritizes rereleases by financial viability rather than pure preservation, the best versions of even the most acclaimed games can slip through the cracks. While I don’t believe its faults make the Famicom release a bad form to play, given that the 1983 version has been rereleased in some form on every generation of Nintendo systems since 2001’s *Doubutso no Mori/Animal Forest* on the Nintendo 64, the sheer volume of availability of this compromised conversion might make it more culturally relevant to many audiences than the original arcade classic ever could, if it even had the chance to. Aside from a minigame in *Donkey Kong 64* (1999), the arcade game was never released on the Wii’s Virtual Console Arcade service or in any other form for decades. While emulation support for the game in MAME dates back to 2007, convenience favors official channels and that convenience informs the history we can engage with firsthand. With its only worldwide release in 2012 and 2014 on the 3DS Virtual Console, even *Donkey Kong Original Edition* is not exempt from this failing, damning it to remain an obscure footnote rather than the renovation it should be credited for.

Ideal forms of a game might not always be a willful choice to promote other versions or leave history behind, though. While copyright issues with Ikegami are a speculative justification for why *Donkey Kong* (1981) and many of Nintendo’s arcade games have seen few or no rereleases, it does mean that a significant part of the company's earliest history is forgotten over time. Thankfully, the sentiment of neglect towards Nintendo’s nascent releases has been shifting. A fully accurate and affordable [*Arcade Archives* release of the original arcade game has been available on the Nintendo eShop](https://www.nintendo.com/us/store/products/arcade-archives-donkey-kong-switch/) since 2018, starting a trend of restoring VS. Arcade System and other titles that hopefully remains constant. Hardware restrictions have always been a factor for availability as well. In today’s age, where even systems from just a generation ago are making significant progress in emulation, if not bypassing emulation altogether through source code decompilation, we’re blessed by options that are exactly what we’re looking for. A version of a game isn’t restrained by just accuracy, but now personal preference, with as many changes and “quality-of-life” improvements that we could ever dream of.

Perhaps that adaptability is a curse of convenience, as it was for me; it's easy to forget that matching different libraries on new hardware is difficult, harrowing, and laborious work. Delving into the Famicom versions' contemporaries on the market reminds me that it could always be worse. That sentiment isn’t to belittle any of the extremely skilled programmers who brought these alternatives to life, but to recognize a noble desire for any artist to do their best. Restricted by scheduling, corporate meddling, and far more limited hardware, these ports are only as good as their effort, and unfortunately, the developers are products of their circumstances. Some home computer versions of the arcade game released by Atarisoft and Ocean Software were at least content-complete, but none of them resemble the arcade game visually. By comparison, other home consoles, such as the Atari 2600, the Intellivision, and the aforementioned ColecoVision, suffered from even poorer accuracy to the original game, with missing content and worse fidelity. Disparity was the standard before 1983, and by the Famicom version being far closer to the arcade version than it appeared at first blush, I found a new appreciation for it that I hadn’t had before.

As the self-proclaimed benchmark for the new hardware, the Family Computer's architects set out to recreate the game that gave Nintendo the relevance it has today as faithfully as they could. The arduous task of leapfrogging the competition by years in price and performance seemed impossible, but with more experience and the advent of memory management controller chips, Uemara-san and his team's efforts would pay dividends. *Pac-Man* (1984), *Ninja Gaiden* (1989), and many other future arcade-to-Famicom titles would be rewarded with greater accuracy than *Donkey Kong* could ever have reached at the time, and in the case of the latter, a completely new experience from the arcades worthy of carving a legacy of its own. *Donkey Kong* may not be ideal, in its adaptations, back in its vogue, or in its quality when experienced today, but it remains fun. The legacy of the Famicom version's superiority to other ports reminds me of how advanced the original arcade game was from the other coin-ops it shared floor space with, and what more poetic way to mark the beginning of a new era for Nintendo than the gaps and girders that elevated it to acclaim to begin with?
